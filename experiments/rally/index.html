Reading the graph

Amber is the longest rally; slate is the generation average. The win count is relative: if both sides improve together, it can stay near even. The scripted-bot benchmark is a separate yardstick. A sharp line is progress, not proof either side has mastered Pong.

Two populations. No referee.

Each side is a population of 16 small neural networks. They play one another, earn fitness from points and returns, and breed separately. Every generation adds a checkpoint against past champions so the contest does not only reward beating today's opponent.

The network

Inputs describe the ball and both paddles from a mirrored left-side view. The tanh output sets paddle velocity between −1× and +1× speed.

Selection and mutation

Each population keeps its top two unchanged. Children come from the top six by single-point crossover; each weight mutates independently with 10% probability. Five percent of children get a 0.6 big jump. Normal mutation shrinks as rallies get longer, with a 0.03 floor.

Fitness

Per agent, fitness is summed over matches: 10 points for each point scored, plus 1 for each successful paddle return. A match ends at 5 points or 1,500 frames. Hall-of-fame challenge returns count toward fitness too.

Benchmark and caveat

The best network from each side is also tested against a fixed bot that tracks the ball at 60% paddle speed. Co-evolution can still stall or cycle; the hall of fame and benchmark help reveal that, but cannot eliminate it.
