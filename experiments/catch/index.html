Reading the graph

Each step along the bottom is one generation. The amber line is the most catches by any single paddle. The blue-grey line is the average across all of them. The graph slides along as new generations arrive.

Early on both lines sit near zero because the networks are random. When one paddle stumbles onto “move toward the ball”, its children inherit it and the whole population jumps within a few generations.

The average stays a little under the best because every child carries random changes, and most random changes make a paddle worse. Raise the mutation in the rules and watch the gap widen.

How a paddle thinks

  1. See. Each paddle reads five numbers: the gap to the ball, the ball’s position and height, its sideways drift, and where the paddle stands.
  2. Decide. A tiny network turns them into one number: push left, push right, or stay put.
  3. Score. A catch earns a point. A miss costs a life, and a near miss earns a little credit so progress can show.
  4. Evolve. The top four carry over unchanged. The rest are children of the top ten, with a few weights changed at random.
